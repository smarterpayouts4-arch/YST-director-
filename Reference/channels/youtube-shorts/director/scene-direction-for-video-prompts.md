## Purpose

This file is the compact working reference for *why a scene should feel true*: visualization before generation, emotional objective, atmosphere, human behavior, tempo-as-purpose, selective detail, audience perspective, and anti-cliché.

It is **not** a shot-grammar book. For framing, axis, continuity, and camera movement, use [`visual-grammar-for-video-prompts.md`](visual-grammar-for-video-prompts.md) (Arijon).

It is **not** a substitute for owning/archiving the 1922 book. It does **not** authorize topic claims.

## 1. Picture Sense — Visualize Before Generating

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** Picture sense is seeing the scene on a mental screen before anyone shoots: the room, the props it actually has, how people dress, where they stand, how they enter and leave, and the hundred small facts that make the beat play. If a scene or the join between scenes rings false in that mental play-through, stop and fix it *before* execution.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** Waiting until the image exists to notice the contradiction wastes the take — and, for us, wastes a generation.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** Play the connected sequence as one continuous mental film. Catch geography, wardrobe, and social-logic breaks between beats.  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Do not write the still until subject, room, wardrobe, props, pose, and spatial relationships are already decided.  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Name start state, the one change, and end state so the motion model is not asked to invent missing facts.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Every scene package.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not treat visualization as permission to invent new story facts the Atom does not contain.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.II “The Picture Sense,” PDF pp.25–26, 29–32.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Picture sense is latent; study develops it but cannot create it from nothing. Refuse to execute until each scene has been mentally screened and, to the best of your knowledge, will read as realistically plausible.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.II, PDF pp.25–26, 29.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** A sequence can be technically tidy and still be false. Milne’s example: a savage denunciation in close shot, then the next full shot has the woman walking on serenely while a crowded ballroom noticed nothing. The script was “technically perfect.” The *picture* was nonsense.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** Polished slides can still fail the mental play-through.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** After each slot, ask what the other people in the room would have seen and done.  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** If the still implies a public confrontation, the room must look like a public confrontation (or the beat must be private).  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Do not hold a calm walk immediately after a rage beat unless you *show* the recovery.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Any join between two scenes, and any scene with more than one person.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not “fix” a false join by adding a new plot incident the Atom never earned.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.II, PDF pp.31–32.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Picture sense is common sense applied to pictures. Literary skill without visualization still fails.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.II, PDF pp.29–30, 32.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

### MODERN SHORTS / AI-VIDEO ADAPTATION

Before writing either prompt, resolve: who, where, wardrobe, hero prop, pose, eyeline, what must exist in the plate, and the intended end-state. The still owns look; the motion prompt must not be asked to invent a room, a second person, or a costume change.

The product’s **exactly 7 scenes / ≈7s** window is an owner contract. Use picture sense to test whether those seven slots form one coherent Short — do not cite 7 or 7s as Milne.

## 2. Emotional Objective and Human Truth

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** Milne’s headline asset is **experience** — specifically a store of **emotional experience** the director can recall as mental pictures and scale to the beat. Feeling the story matters; atmosphere and visualization matter; they do not replace that store. Scale a comprehended emotion (suffering, sacrifice, joy) up or down to *this* moment. Do not require living the exact incident. Do not manufacture experience to “complete an arc.” Physical research is secondary to emotional content. The arc diagram is imaginary, not science.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** A technically pretty scene with the wrong size of feeling reads as fake.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** Name the emotion in ordinary words, and its size (schoolboy stammer ≠ Napoleon on St. Helena).  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Choose face, posture, and room that match that size — not a stock “cinematic anguish” face for a small beat.  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Prefer **one** visible change that carries the feeling. Do not stack grief + rage + revelation.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Every scene.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not require the *operator* to have lived the emotion.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.I “The Great and the Less Great,” PDF pp.12–14, 19–23 (p.13: EXPERIENCE in capitals).  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH for the idea; LOW for the park-bench / vagabond biography as a method.

**PRINCIPLE:** Conceit (my suffering was greater than Napoleon’s) produces worse work. Scale honestly.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.I, PDF p.20.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** After emotional understanding come the “little touches” that get under the skin, plus atmosphere. Those are later chapters — they do not replace feeling the scene.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.I, PDF pp.22–23.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

### MODERN SHORTS / AI-VIDEO ADAPTATION

Write the emotion as **visible behavior**, not as a vibe adjective (“sad cinematic woman”). Scene 1 craft (look-down → look-up) is an example of sized feeling, not a content template. Do not import magnesium / Nighttime Woman.

## 3. Atmosphere Through Suggestion

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** After sincere characterization and direct story-telling, **atmosphere** does more than anything else to make an audience *accept* what it sees. The aim is the *effect* of the place, not a literal copy of a real room. Literal reproduction can look unconvincing; implication and suggestion can feel more real.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** Generic “nice interior” does not make a beat believable.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** Name the feeling the room must do (lived-in night, public exposure, private doubt).  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** A few specific, researched surfaces beat a catalog of furniture.  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** One small bit of life in the room (a curtain, a held object) can certify the whole sequence — if it does not steal the face.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Every environment that is more than a blank void.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not puzzle the viewer with atmosphere so “artistic” that the story disappears (Milne’s warning on *Caligari*-style futurism).  
**SOURCE:** Ch.VII Rex Ingram on “Atmosphere,” PDF pp.69–72.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Study the place enough that someone who *lives* there would recognize it — a beach-comber’s shanty, a factory, a restaurant. Small authentic bits (a sign, a type of person, a habitual object) create color. Ingram: little flashes of life, on screen only a moment, can give reality to an entire sequence. In screening character-driven material, atmosphere and characterization can matter more than incident.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.VII, PDF pp.69–71.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH. Reject the chapter’s racialized “white man / black giant” example as **HISTORICAL — NOT DOCTRINE**.

**PRINCIPLE:** Suggestion can be carried too far. If the audience is puzzled instead of convinced, you have failed.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.VII, PDF pp.71–72.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Even careful atmosphere will be nitpicked. Casting a real doctor as a doctor does not automatically look “right” to doctors. Plausibility is an effect, not a credential.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.VII, PDF pp.72–73.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** MEDIUM (anecdote); useful as a warning against “add a real expert and stop thinking.”

### MODERN SHORTS / AI-VIDEO ADAPTATION

Models default to hotel-room / stock-kitchen / golden-hour generic. Prefer Atom-specific room facts. Do not stuff the prompt with extra extras “for atmosphere.” One lived-in prop beats a crowd.

## 4. Dramatic Hierarchy — Story Before Spectacle

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** No spectacle succeeds if the director forgets the personal story in the bigness of the background. He must step from the mountain to the molehill without smashing the molehill. Mass scenes with no dramatic purpose die; the audience starts asking what the whole thing means. Money, costumes, and “classy” ballrooms do not rescue a neglected human story.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** Impressive pictures that lose the person lose the Short.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** If a slot is “big,” keep a named human action as the center.  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Do not spend the plate on architecture while the face is a speck — unless the beat *is* scale crushing the person, and even then the person must remain readable.  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Do not request crowds, parades, or “epic” motion for texture.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Any temptation to make a scene look expensive.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not ban a wide shot when geography is the point — just do not let geography replace the beat.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.IX “Mountains and Molehills,” PDF pp.87–93.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Intimate glimpses inside a large event are what bring the big scene to an emotional climax. Small human details (even ones critics mock) can be the right track.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.IX, PDF pp.88, 93.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH. Do not inherit Griffith’s racialized war examples as content.

**PRINCIPLE:** Pictorial beauty that does not serve story is a failed experiment. Tourneur’s *The Blue Bird* is Milne’s caution: artistry without audience grip. Lift the *feeling* of a place; do not replace the story with decoration.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXI, PDF pp.189–190.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH for the caution; the 1922 box-office moral is period.

### MODERN SHORTS / AI-VIDEO ADAPTATION

“Cinematic,” “epic lighting,” “holy atmosphere,” orbiting cameras, and extra people are the modern mountain. Default to one person, one room, one job.

## 5. Character and Performance Direction

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** Sincere, true characterization makes pictures attractive. Most work stays on the surface. Develop a character through **commonplace, recognizable behavior** — the things a viewer might say “I did that yesterday” about — not only murder, betrayal, or glory. Audiences often prefer people like themselves to glorified heroes and too-pretty heroines.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** Generic “ideal influencer” faces are the modern superior-hero problem.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** Specify what this person *does* that is particular, not a type-label.  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Ordinary posture, specific hands, a real task. Avoid stock “hero stare.”  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Small, complete human actions.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Almost every Shorts beat.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not invent a colorful backstory the Atom does not contain.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XII Frank Borzage, PDF pp.116–118.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH. Period ethnic “types” in the *Humoresque* discussion are **HISTORICAL — NOT DOCTRINE**.

**PRINCIPLE:** Minor characters may be developed only if they do **not** crowd the plot or the important people.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XII, PDF p.117.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Authors still thinking in print miss what pictures can show. Think in visible behavior.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XII, PDF p.117.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Showing a player the business can help; so can *not* showing them (De Mille vs Neilan). Either way, the goal is a living performance, not a cast of mechanical imitations of the director. Enthusiasm and confidence produce better work than puppetry.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXVI, PDF pp.224–227.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** MEDIUM for method; HIGH for “do not over-direct into imitation.”

**PRINCIPLE:** State the **what** and the feeling to convey; do not model a single canonical **how** unless the story locks the action. Mimicry of one posed gesture produces wooden copies. If a bit of business reads false, change the condition (a different object, a different task) rather than forcing the pose. Every person in the frame should be distinctly themselves.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** Over-specified acting direction is how prompts become cliché faces.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** Intent + outcome per slot, not a pose catalog.  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Emotional goal and relationship, not “hand on brow, cinematic tear.”  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Motivational trigger and destination; leave path variation unless Atom fixes the action.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Character beats with agency.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** When the Atom already locks a specific ritual or clue action.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.V Cecil De Mille, PDF pp.53–56, 59.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH. Studio briefing anecdotes stay historical.

### MODERN SHORTS / AI-VIDEO ADAPTATION

Name concrete action and expression. Ban vague acting adjectives and silent-film pantomime (hand-to-brow, knitting-mother, mustache villain). One person per clip unless the Atom requires two.

## 6. Tempo and Beat Energy

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** Tempo is the proper timing of episodes — a music word that applies to pictures. Chase/urgency wants the fastest tempo that still stays probable. Ceremony or a heavy denunciation wants slow. Other beats sit on a scale between. Even a hair’s-breadth difference matters. The audience may not *notice* the variation; they feel it.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** Uniform “medium energy” is padding.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** Assign each of the seven slots a purpose-tempo: hold / gather / strike / release — from the story, not from a clock.  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** A still can already look mid-motion or at rest. Choose which.  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Match motion amplitude to the beat. Slow must be justified. Fast must stay readable.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Every scene.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not import silent-film camera-crank speeds, chase-comedy recipes, or serial “overshooting” as product law.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XIII “What Tempo Means in Directing,” PDF pp.125–129.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Slow tempo on unimportant scenes reads as filler. The spectator decides the picture is padded. Rapid scenes are easier to judge; slow scenes take more skill. A drama that sustains interest through a series of episodes and then bursts at the climax is a drama timed on purpose. Scenes that must *register* a strong point are often played a little **slower than life** so the moment lands.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XIII, PDF pp.126–127.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** A player can spoil a beat by leaving a room too slowly or too quickly, or by changing expression too soon or too late. In comedy, a slight tempo error kills the laugh. Cheap chase pictures fail because speed is inconsistent from shot to shot.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XIII, PDF pp.128–129.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH for the idea; the slapstick examples are period.

**PRINCIPLE:** Lack of attention to tempo produces **overshooting** — too much footage on a beat that has already landed. (Serial production is period mechanics.)  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XIV, PDF pp.133–135 (tempo overflow only; serial pages are historical).  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** MEDIUM (filtered).

### MODERN SHORTS / AI-VIDEO ADAPTATION

≈7 seconds is the **product design window**, not a Milne timing unit. Use Milne to decide whether that window should be almost still, one small action, or a clear strike. Do not pack walk + sit + speak + reveal into one slot. External renderers will not hit 7.000s; the package still *targets* that window.

## 7. Art Direction and Pictorial Balance

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** Settings used to be dumped full of mismatched furniture. Accuracy and composition now matter. Many exteriors (and interiors) fail because they lack **pictorial balance**. A strong dramatic director may still be weak at composition — someone must still decide the picture.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** A beautiful mess still fights the face.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** One environment per scene unless a justified move.  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Foreground / subject / background in a readable hierarchy. Period and class of objects must agree.  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Do not let motion invent a second, prettier room.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Every plate.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not chase mural-level beauty that starves the story (see §4 / *Blue Bird*).  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXI, PDF pp.188–189.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Seek the art director’s eye on exteriors as well as interiors. Composition faults are common outdoors.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXI, PDF pp.188–189.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH as a reminder; we have no art-department workflow.

## 8. Selective Detail

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** The right amount of story detail is a rare skill. Superfluous detail that is “interesting in itself” knocks the director off the main track. Knowing where a little prolongation will *help* is a separate art.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** Prompt bloat is the modern superfluity.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** Add a detail only where it helps the spine. Suspense may use several *earned* interruptions; decoration may use none. “One per scene” is an operator heuristic, not Milne.  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Include a prop only if it does a job (identity, atmosphere, or the next beat).  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Do not introduce a new object mid-clip “for interest.”  
**WHEN TO USE:** When the spine is already clear.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not decorate because the model can render nouns.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXV “Illustrating the Use of Detail,” PDF pp.218–219.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** When the audience already knows the outcome, **judicious detail** can make them forget they know it — interruptions that delay a confession, small physical events that fray nerves — so they live inside the moment instead of skipping to the ending. That is detail in service of suspense, not clutter.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXV, PDF pp.219–220.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH. Do not inherit the “fallen woman / last-minute electric chair” plot as a template.

**PRINCIPLE:** A close, humanizing detail (hesitating over the correct fork) can carry a social predicament better than another spectacular reel. Lubitsch, in Milne’s telling, was amazed by that kind of American detail-work; Milne treats the amazement as a *gap*, not a virtue. Prefer **suggestion** of emotion over obvious registration. Do not claim subtlety and then hammer the same point with a ladder of near-identical close looks.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXIII, PDF pp.203–208.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH for suggestion / anti-hammer. Period “German vs American director” nationalism is **HISTORICAL — NOT DOCTRINE**.

## 9. Audience Perspective / Fresh-Eyes Test

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** When a beat is handled falsely, detect it and change it before execution. Neilan treats this **dramatic sense** as the same faculty as picture sense. He would place sitting in the audience’s chair — neutral, critical, first-time eyes — *ahead* of those craft reflexes. Stay inside what a first-time viewer can follow. Going five years ahead (or five years behind) both fail. Keep a few paces ahead — not afield.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** Creators who know the Atom by heart skip the sentence the stranger needs.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** Read the seven scenes as if you had only seen prior slots.  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** The still must be readable without a caption explaining who this is.  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Do not require knowledge that scene 6 will supply.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Every handoff between scenes, and the Short as a whole.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not inherit Milne’s 1922 claim that the average audience is “in the teens” as current demographic law.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXVI Marshall Neilan, PDF pp.227–229.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH for perspective; LOW for 1922 audience-age claims.

**PRINCIPLE:** Directors who live with a picture too long lose first-fresh perspective. They cut valuable story points because *they* already know them. The audience sees the story once, in ignorance, until it begins. Every point of value must be retained. Loss of perspective is one reason pictures feel jumpy and discontinuous. Jump outside yourself and look as a layman.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XVII, PDF p.159; Ch.XXVI, PDF pp.229–230.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Dramatic sense is the ability to know when a situation is handled falsely and to change it. Neilan and Milne treat this as the same faculty as visualization.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXVI, PDF pp.227–228.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Above all, appreciation of the ordinary human side of life. Without it, technique does not save the work.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXVI, PDF p.230.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

## 10. Avoiding Directorial and Generative Clichés

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** Directors adopt false conventions by repeating them until they feel like life. Oversight conventions (cigar freshly lit, then a butt; brown derby exit, black derby entrance) are the small continuity lies fans already catch. Worse are **movie-only objects and behaviors**: the library wall-safe, the doll-dressed telephone, the pistol in every desk drawer, the breakfast table paved with silver, college rooms wallpapered with pennants and pipes, burlesque “rubes,” mining-camp Pollyannas, the knitting tearful mother vs the freak social-climber mother. Mention them to a director and he laughs — then uses them the next day.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** The generative model has the same habit.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** If a prop or behavior exists only because “movies always do it,” cut it.  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Prefer Atom-true objects.  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Prefer plausible human business.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Every draft.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not replace 1922 clichés with a new mandatory set of “anti-cliché” props.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXII “Directorial Conventions,” PDF pp.194–197.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH. Tobacco-as-villain-sign and Prohibition jokes are **HISTORICAL — NOT DOCTRINE** (the *anti-cliché method* remains).

**PRINCIPLE:** Stereotype groups (college snobs, small-town rubes, western queens, impossible mothers) are not people. Normal people reach the screen too rarely.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXII, PDF pp.196–197.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH. Do not inherit the chapter’s gender/class sneer; inherit the demand for plausible humans.

### MODERN SHORTS / AI-VIDEO ADAPTATION

Do not let the model default to generic cinematic clichés when a more specific Atom-consistent choice exists. Modern twins of Milne’s list: golden-hour kitchen, floating particles, slow-mo hair, orbit cam, “cinematic tear,” stock wellness bedroom, hero-product glow.

## 11. Story → Image Translation

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** The continuity (the pictured plan) is the beginning of the *picture* form. The original story is not yet a picture. The writer who thinks in pictures finishes with a completed picture in mind and, if the work is good, on paper. The director who can visualize discerns that picture and transfers it to the medium. Direction is closer to **translation / recreation** than to inventing a new story.  
**WHY IT MATTERS:** Our Atom is the story source. Prompts must not rewrite it.  
**STORYBOARD APPLICATION:** Each slot states the Atom fact it makes visible.  
**IMAGE-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Translate the beat into room + body + object + light. No new claims.  
**MOTION-PROMPT APPLICATION:** Translate the same beat into one action.  
**WHEN TO USE:** Always.  
**WHEN NOT TO USE:** Do not treat 1922 studio hierarchy (who “creates”) as our org chart.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XVII “Who Creates a Picture?,” PDF pp.157–160.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH for translation; the writer-vs-director score-settling is period.

**PRINCIPLE:** Before production, list the interiors/exteriors the story actually needs, and which scenes play in each. Know what must exist (set, props, wardrobe, location that can hold the action) before anyone shoots. Combining two rooms to save a set is a production economy — the durable idea is **coherence**, not cheapness.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.III “Preparation for Production,” PDF pp.34–36.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH for the pre-list; studio casting/temperament anecdotes are **HISTORICAL**.

### MODERN SHORTS / AI-VIDEO ADAPTATION

Still plate first: subject, environment, shot size (from Arijon), composition, lighting, empty sector if text will be burned later, no readable text in the image. The plate must already contain wardrobe, hero object, and room so motion cannot invent them.

## 12. Story → Motion Translation

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** Visualize continuous action *before* the camera turns. Tempo, performance timing, and small bits of business are how feeling becomes motion. Ingram’s “flashes of life” and Edward Dillon’s walk-out / expression-change examples (quoted in the tempo chapter) are motion direction, not camera recipes.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.II, PDF pp.26, 29; Ch.VII, PDF p.71; Ch.XIII, PDF pp.128–129.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Overload of pictorial movement (mobs, spectacle) without a personal motion at the center is the mountain problem in time.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.IX, PDF pp.87–91.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

### MODERN SHORTS / AI-VIDEO ADAPTATION

One subject action, one camera move (or locked), start/end state, identity and geometry locks. ≈7s is the design window. Arijon decides *which* camera move; Milne decides *whether* the move is earned by feeling and tempo.

## 13. Useful Cross-Scene Principles

### MILNE-DERIVED PRINCIPLE

**PRINCIPLE:** Visualize the *sequence*, not only the isolated scene. The ballroom denunciation fails at the join.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.II, PDF pp.31–32.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH.

**PRINCIPLE:** Continuity of objects and dress is already a convention directors violate (hat color, cigar length). Treat those as direction failures, not style.  
**SOURCE:** Ch.XXII, PDF p.194.  
**CONFIDENCE / APPLICABILITY:** HIGH. Shot-axis / match-triad continuity is Arijon — do not re-teach it here.

**PRINCIPLE:** Living with the material too long creates jumps: you cut the sentence the stranger still needs. They see it once, in ignorance, until it begins. Jump outside yourself and look as a layman.
