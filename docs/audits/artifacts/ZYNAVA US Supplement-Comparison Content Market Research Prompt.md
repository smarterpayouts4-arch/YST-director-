# ZYNAVA US Supplement-Comparison Content Market Research Prompt

## 1. ROLE AND EXPERTISE
Act as a senior US consumer-health market researcher, search-intent analyst, platform strategist, and compliance-aware content strategist. Investigate the shopper’s decision moment first: an adult has already chosen a supplement category, is confronted with conflicting claims and multiple forms, and needs to know what can be compared in plain English before purchase—without being told what is medically right for them.

Your task is to determine whether a comparison-first educational content strategy centered on supplement forms can earn qualified attention and lead appropriate shoppers to ZYNAVA’s guided comparison experience. Treat health, efficacy, safety, and individualized-suitability claims as high-risk. This is market and content research, not medical advice or product recommendation.

## 2. COMPANY CONTEXT
[OWNER-CONFIRMED CONTEXT]
ZYNAVA is a United States online dietary-supplement search and comparison experience. It helps adult shoppers who are overwhelmed by conflicting supplement claims compare forms and relevant label details through education and guided comparison. It is positioned as comparison-first education, not a single-brand supplement storefront.

The priority customer moment is after a shopper has selected a supplement category but before purchase, when they are comparing forms within that category (for example, magnesium forms). The intended viewer reward is plain-English clarity about how forms differ and which label details are worth comparing before choosing. After the education, the intended commercial next step is to start a guided comparison for the supplement category already under consideration or explore an educational guide.

[CONTEXT + CONFLICT]
Evidence may show that supplement shoppers search for specific forms and form comparisons, but it is unclear whether form is the strongest and most understandable lead topic, whether it creates enough value to move shoppers into a guided comparison, and whether YouTube is the best single primary platform for this pre-purchase behavior. Research must investigate this rather than assume it.

[STORY LENS / CATEGORY-OF-ONE]
Evaluate an educational angle of: “Decode the comparison—not the cure.” ZYNAVA should help a shopper see what is comparable on labels and across forms in a specific supplement category, while making clear that form is one factor rather than a universal shortcut to the right personal choice. This is distinct from generic supplement education, influencer-style wellness advice, and product rankings.

## 3. OWNER-CONFIRMED DECISIONS
[OWNER-CONFIRMED DECISIONS]
1. Audience: adults in the United States researching supplements who want clearer evidence before choosing a product.
2. Customer problem: shoppers face conflicting claims and are uncertain which information matters when choosing a product.
3. Offer: a supplement search and comparison experience with educational guidance designed to help people compare supplement forms.
4. Differentiation: comparison-first education rather than a single-brand storefront, with a stated focus on supplement forms.
5. Priority moment: intercept shoppers after category selection and before purchase, while comparing forms of the same supplement.
6. Viewer reward: explain, in plain English, how forms differ and which label details are worth comparing.
7. Business bridge: invite interested viewers to start a guided comparison for their chosen supplement category or explore educational guides.
8. Geography: United States online.

[RESTRICTIONS — NON-NEGOTIABLE]
1. Explain what supplement forms and comparable label details are; do not state which form a person should choose.
2. Do not provide medical advice, diagnoses, dosage recommendations, or individualized form recommendations.
3. Do not connect a form recommendation to a symptom, condition, treatment goal, or dosage need.
4. Present form as one comparison factor alongside relevant product-label information—not as a shortcut to the right choice.
5. When a personal health decision is implicated, direct the shopper to an appropriate clinician or pharmacist.
6. Do not present ZYNAVA as a medical authority or individual product recommender.

[PROVENANCE NOTE]
The business and audience statements above are owner-confirmed. The restrictions are owner-imposed. Do not upgrade hypotheses into facts.

## 4. WORKING HYPOTHESES
[WORKING HYPOTHESES — TEST, DO NOT ASSUME]
1. YouTube may be the strongest single primary platform because its evergreen, search-led explainer format may fit active pre-purchase comparison behavior and allow enough context for responsible form and label comparison.
2. Plain-English, category-specific explainers framed as “What should I compare when looking at [supplement] forms?” may attract and qualify shoppers.
3. Form-led education may be more compelling than generic label-comparison or product-quality education for some high-confusion supplement categories.
4. Shoppers may value form-led educational comparison enough to move from content into a guided comparison.
5. The viable content pattern may be: distinguish forms, identify relevant comparable label details, state the limits of form as a decision shortcut, and invite a category-specific guided comparison.

Treat each hypothesis as falsifiable. State what evidence supports it, weakens it, or leaves it unresolved.

## 5. RESEARCH QUESTIONS
Answer each question separately and with a direct decision implication.

1. Which US supplement categories have the strongest combination of current search demand, form-related confusion, pre-purchase comparison intent, and feasible non-medical educational coverage? Identify and prioritize a short list; do not assume magnesium is the winner.
2. For the prioritized categories, what exact language do US shoppers use when searching or discussing forms, label details, comparisons, and product quality—and is “form” understood and compelling enough to lead the content?
3. In the target decision moment, what information do shoppers actually compare before purchase, and where does form rank relative to label details, price, brand, third-party testing, ingredient quantity, claims, reviews, or other factors?
4. Which label details can be consistently compared across the shortlisted categories in a factual, understandable, non-medical way, and which details are too variable, ambiguous, or medically adjacent to use as recurring educational content?
5. Which audience segments are most likely to seek category-specific form comparisons, what trigger creates that search, and what non-medical information gap are they trying to resolve?
6. Is YouTube the best single primary platform for ZYNAVA’s objective when judged against target-audience behavior, search discoverability, evergreen value, authority-building potential, production capacity, traffic quality, and likely guided-comparison conversion? Name the recommended platform even if it is not YouTube.
7. Which direct, adjacent, and aspirational competitors already serve this information need, and what whitespace can ZYNAVA credibly own through “decode the comparison—not the cure”?
8. What proof, sourcing practices, review process, and subject-matter expertise would be required for ZYNAVA’s comparison education to earn trust without implying clinical authority?
9. Does ZYNAVA’s likely guided-comparison destination appear capable of fulfilling the educational promise for the priority categories? Specify the minimum experience requirements and any promise-to-product gaps that would make a content launch premature.
10. What one primary conversion event should define qualified content success for this model, and what supporting indicators should diagnose whether failure is due to reach, relevance, trust, destination fit, or conversion friction?

## 6. EVIDENCE AND RED-TEAM REQUIREMENTS
[EVIDENCE STANDARD]
Use current, US-relevant evidence wherever possible. Cite every material external claim with a direct URL, publisher/source, publication or access date, and a concise explanation of what the source establishes. Prioritize: first-party search/platform data where available; Google Trends and search-result observations; reputable keyword-demand or SERP data with methodology disclosed; YouTube, Google, Reddit, retailer-review, and community-language observations clearly labeled as directional rather than representative; FDA, FTC, NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, USP, NSF, ConsumerLab or similarly authoritative sources for regulatory, labeling, or substantiation context; and credible consumer surveys or market research with sample/methodology stated.

Do not make efficacy, safety, absorption, bioavailability, condition, symptom, treatment, or dosage claims unless directly necessary to describe a source—and do not turn such evidence into consumer-facing recommendations. Flag material substantiation uncertainty. Do not treat search volume, social engagement, retailer reviews, or anecdotal posts as proof of consumer prevalence or medical truth.

[DISCONFIRMING-EVIDENCE REQUIREMENT]
Actively seek evidence that disproves or limits the strategy, including evidence that: form is not a commonly used or understood search concept; broader quality or label questions outrank form comparisons; shoppers prefer retail, clinician, pharmacist, or brand-led sources; YouTube underperforms another platform for this intent; the available evidence cannot support simple form comparisons without creating misleading inferences; audience interest does not translate into guided-comparison intent; or ZYNAVA’s destination cannot fulfill the promise.

For each major conclusion, include confidence (high/medium/low), key limitations, and the strongest counter-evidence. Clearly distinguish observed evidence, owner-confirmed decisions, working hypotheses, research findings, and unresolved questions.

[COMPETITOR CLASSIFICATION]
Classify competitors before evaluating them:
- Direct: supplement comparison, search, or decision-support experiences competing for the same pre-purchase comparison task.
- Adjacent: supplement brands, retailers, marketplaces, publishers, review/testing services, clinicians, pharmacists, or communities that address part of the decision journey.
- Aspirational: trusted comparison, consumer-education, or decision-aid brands outside this exact category whose clarity, trust model, or content-to-tool bridge is worth learning from.

[OPPORTUNITY SCORING]
Score each candidate category/platform/content opportunity from 1–5 on: Demand, Audience Relevance, ZYNAVA Authority/Credibility, Operational Feasibility, and Compliance/Reputational Risk. Define the scoring rubric, show weighted and unweighted results, explain the weights, and include a short rationale grounded in evidence. Higher risk must reduce the final priority score.

Do not dump raw CSV, large unprocessed keyword lists, or unsupported competitor lists. Synthesize the evidence into decisions.

## 7. REQUIRED REPORT STRUCTURE
Produce a decision-ready report in the following structure.

1. Executive decision
- State the recommended strategic direction in no more than five bullets.
- Name the recommended first supplement category or categories, the recommended single primary platform, the core educational angle, the CTA hypothesis, and the primary success event.
- State the most important reason not to proceed as proposed, if applicable.

2. Provenance and decision frame
- Separate: owner-confirmed decisions; restrictions; working hypotheses; externally observed findings; and unresolved questions.
- Restate the shopper tension and the central contest: whether form-led comparison is a valuable and safe entry point versus broader comparison education.

3. Audience, demand, and language evidence
- Describe priority audience segments, decision triggers, information gaps, and exact wording patterns.
- Compare form-led demand and language with broader label-quality/product-comparison demand.
- Include a concise evidence table with source, finding, interpretation, limitation, and confidence.

4. Category prioritization
- Assess a focused shortlist of supplement categories.
- Include the 1–5 opportunity scorecard for demand, relevance, authority, feasibility, and risk; show final priority ranking.
- Specify why the top category or categories should be tested first and why lower-ranked categories should wait.

5. Comparison-scope and trust guardrails
- Identify label details that are feasible for factual recurring comparison, category by category where relevant.
- Identify language, claims, comparison frames, and content areas to avoid because they could imply medical advice, individualized suitability, efficacy, safety, or dosage guidance.
- Specify minimum source standards, reviewer roles, update cadence, disclosures, and escalation rules needed to publish responsibly.

6. Platform decision
- Compare YouTube against the most credible alternatives for the stated audience and objective.
- Recommend exactly one primary platform, with evidence, trade-offs, and conditions that would reverse the decision.

7. Competitive landscape and whitespace
- Present direct, adjacent, and aspirational competitors in separate groups.
- For each meaningful competitor, summarize audience/job served, content or tool approach, trust signals, limitation, and implication for ZYNAVA.
- Define a credible category-of-one position: “decode the comparison—not the cure.”

8. Content strategy: three pillars and six experiments only
- Recommend exactly 3 content pillars, each anchored to a specific customer tension and the selected platform.
- For each pillar, provide exactly 2 experiments, for a total of 6 experiments.
- For every experiment include: target segment; category; audience question; educational promise; compliant comparison boundary; CTA hypothesis; destination; primary metric; secondary diagnostic metrics; success criterion; failure criterion; required evidence/review; and the decision it will inform.
- Keep the work category-specific and comparison-first. Do not create generic wellness content, product rankings, individualized recommendations, or a long list of disconnected topics.

9. Measurement and validation plan
- Define one primary success event, preferably a qualified guided-comparison start if the destination and tracking can support it; recommend another event only if evidence shows it is more valid.
- Define event qualification, attribution window, supporting metrics, instrumentation requirements, benchmarks or initial test thresholds, and interpretation rules.
- Distinguish success, inconclusive evidence, and failure.

10. Red-team findings and go/no-go conditions
- Present the strongest case against the strategy.
- List the assumptions that remain unproven.
- Set explicit go, revise, and no-go conditions, including destination-readiness and compliance-readiness gates.

11. Source appendix
- List all sources with links, dates, source type, and a one-line note on use and limitations.

Use tables where they improve comparison, but keep the analysis synthesized and decision-oriented.
