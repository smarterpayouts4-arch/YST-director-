# Story principles

The Atom owns what is true. This brain owns how that truth becomes one short-form story.

## Operating sequence

1. Understand the Atom as closed-world truth.
2. Decide the story.
3. Write seven scenes that obey that decision.

Story first. Scenes second. Do not improvise hierarchy, Hook, carrier, and wording at the same time.

## Scene construction order

For every scene, decide Role, then Purpose, then situationLock, then Scene Description, then Narration, then On-Screen Text. Each stage builds on the one before it. Do not write a later field before deciding the earlier one. Role is a short name, an em dash, then one sentence defining what this story function means in THIS story (never a bare label, slot number, or forced taxonomy). Purpose is two to four dense sentences: what the Role means for this beat, the specific viewer-change it causes, and what evidence this beat has earned the right to show — never a topic label; Scene Description must dramatize it. situationLock is a semantic contract (`eventMode` first — physical_comparison, mediated_surface, or dual — then required subjects, visible action or state, relationship; location and human presence default to open — open marks the staged place/presence as P1B’s free non-claim choice). It is not a shot card. P1C reads `eventMode` and must not infer channel from Scene Description. Narration advances the viewer’s understanding aloud — it must not caption or restate the scene description. Job definitions stay with N / T / V.

## Principles

- **Audience first.** Anchor in the viewer’s real decision, comparison, misunderstanding, noticing, or task — not an analyst abstraction.
- **One connected story.** Seven scenes are one Short, not seven related facts and not a research paraphrase.
- **Story lens.** Prefer the Atom’s strongest useful way of seeing the subject over the most generic explanation. Do not invent a marketing angle the Atom does not support.
- **Dance, not list.** Connect beats with consequence or contrast (therefore / but behavior). Answering an open question must open the next; only Scene 7 closes clean. Do not write an “and then” fact sequence.
- **Write toward the ending.** Decide the payoff before Scene 1 so Scene 7 can resolve the opening. The payoff must pass a retell test: one two-beat line — setup, then turn — a viewer could repeat to a friend tomorrow, using none of the Atom's nouns. Remove unnecessary payoff hedging. Make the ending decisive, but never strengthen the Atom’s claim. Prefer a direct rule over phrases such as “can actually” when the qualification adds no truth. The second payoff beat must add a new move, consequence, or decision — not merely restate the first beat. If both beats communicate the same idea, compress them. Noun repeat is allowed when it keeps the claim clear. This is not a replay-loop last-line trick.
- **Spoken first-hearing language.** The Atom gives facts, not wording — rebuild every spoken line from scratch. Preserve the claim — never the sentence, and never the Atom's name for a thing: call each recurring thing by what the viewer sees or does with it, not by a compound noun. Write `openingQuestion`, `payoff`, beat jobs, and `hookWhy` as you would say them. Narration: one new idea, at most two sentences and 22 words. A definition never travels with its implication. A fact appears only in the scene where it changes what the viewer understands. Frame as a realization unfolding, not a verdict announced. Avoid “X is not a Y”, “X is the unit/measure/term that…”, passive verdicts, and three-technical-noun clauses. Internal planning may use precise analytical concepts; spoken story must translate them. In spoken fields, avoid like-for-like, like with like, on that basis, fair basis — say the consequence in ordinary words. Ordinary words first. Translate Atom research phrasing — do not recite it. If a technical term is required, explain it in the same breath. Speak to one viewer. Not a children’s-show voice. Not a label-instructor voice. No banned-word lists. Formal→spoken rewrite pairs teach the move across domains (travel clock, quote scope, dated accounts) — copy the transformation, never the nouns.
- **Teach through the concrete.** When a supported example makes the idea understandable, the viewer should meet that real thing early enough to learn the idea through it.
- **N / T / V do different jobs.** Narration speaks. On-screen text labels or teases. Scene description shows a concrete visual situation that keeps the scene’s open question visible. Do not invent a proxy object merely because an abstract method is hard to visualize; keep objects the Atom or locked story actually names. Do not make all three say the same thing. Scene description is creative visual intent, not camera or generation syntax.
- **Scene realization.** Scene Description is the strongest visible story event that expresses the Purpose in this particular beat, staged as a robust scene the viewer could watch. First decide: what is the story event, rather than the information being communicated? Prefer direct human/object/world action over explanatory representations when both communicate the same meaning. A screen, interface, document, chart, card, or diagram is appropriate only when it is itself part of the story event. Preserve the current unresolved question and reveal only the evidence this beat has earned. Before finalizing Scene Description, ask: am I showing the story event or explaining the information? Robustness: as many sentences as the moment needs — typically four to seven — consistent with situationLock — one committed lived-in place; who is present and what they do; the subjects in concrete visible form; mood and tension as felt atmosphere (not a lighting recipe); what stays unresolved. A one-line caption is a defect; a sentence that repeats another field or adds no new visible detail is also a defect — density, never padding. If a sentence merely states what a label says, rewrite it as a person’s action around that information. Place, people, and mood are non-claim staging, never new facts. Audience and customerMoment say why the viewer cares; they do not choose the visual channel. Reserve generic for unnamed people only — never as a product adjective. Do not write camera, lighting, or material-finish recipes here. Three-mode staging follows the locked `eventMode`: physical_comparison stages the physical alternatives, not a listing or device; mediated_surface may show the named surface; dual only when Purpose named both.

- **About seven seconds per scene.** One clear piece of story work. Later scenes answer prior uncertainty, reveal the next useful distinction, or move toward payoff. Vary rhythm. Do not deliver seven equally dense explanations.
- **Payoff resolves the opening.** Scene 7 answers the question, assumption, decision, tension, or promise planted in Scene 1. Do not fabricate advice beyond the Atom.
- **Truth stays closed.** Do not invent facts, examples, outcomes, customers, or drama. Do not invent product-part locations, packaging geography, or store layout the Atom does not name. If the Atom names a panel or field, point to that only when `eventMode` is mediated_surface or dual and this beat has earned that surface — never on a Scene 1 withhold comparison. Do not invent where else a number lives. If a desired beat needs unsupported information, change the treatment.

## Not these

- Seven informational cards plus a summary
- Forced emotion, first person, character, or controversy unless the Atom supports them
- Platform CTAs, hashtags, or a second Short
